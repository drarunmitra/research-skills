# IJMR language reference

The house register of the *Indian Journal of Medical Research* before 2019, as
observed in a corpus of 14 open-access articles read in full text.

Every rule below carries the excerpt that warrants it. Nothing here is invented.
Where an excerpt shows something that should **not** be imitated, it is marked
as such in the final section.

# Part 1. Sentence templates, by IMRaD slot

## 1.1 The objective closes the Introduction

First person, plain verb, no throat-clearing. The objective is the last thing
the reader meets before Material & Methods.

> "We undertook this study to determine the presence of depression, anxiety and
> stress among medical undergraduates studying in a premier medical institution
> in Odisha (eastern India) and their socio-demographic correlates were also
> assessed." (#1)

> "The aims of this study were to evaluate the epidemiological, clinical and
> laboratory features of brucellosis in patients with cytopenia and the
> complications associated with the disease, and to compare the antibiotic
> combinations used, duration of treatment, normalization time of cytopenia,
> length of hospital stay (LOS) and cure or relapse in brucellosis patients."
> (#2)

> "The aim of this review is to provide an overview of the impact of obesity on
> OA in terms of clinical and imaging aspects, pathogenesis and joint
> replacement outcomes." (#3)

> "We present here an overview of Zika virus (ZiV) in the context to India."
> (#4)

Use: `We undertook this study to …`, `The aim(s) of this study were to …`,
`The aim of this review is to …`, `We present here an overview of …`.

Do not use: "This paper seeks to explore", "Herein, we aim to elucidate",
"The present work endeavours to".

## 1.2 Material & Methods opens with place, then time

One sentence. Institution, city, state, country, then the study window.

> "This study was conducted in the Institute of Medical Sciences at
> Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India, during July-August 2012." (#1)

> "This study included all patients admitted to these two centres in Diyarbakir
> Province, Turkey, for brucellosis-associated cytopenia between 2009 and
> 2013." (#2)

Case reports use the same skeleton, with the patient first:

> "A 12 yr old boy presented to the outpatient department of Orthopaedics,
> Pandit Bhagwat Dayal Sharma Postgraduate Institute of Medical Sciences,
> Rohtak, India, in June 2016, with deformity of both the elbows with flexion
> contractures." (#10)

> "A 23 yr old woman presented to the department of Gastroenterology at Bombay
> Hospital, Mumbai, India, in July 2015 with an acute onset, sharp right
> hypochondriac pain; intermittent fever and gradually progressive symmetrical
> abdominal distension for the preceding two weeks." (#11)

## 1.3 Ethics is one flat sentence

No hedging, no explanation of why ethics matter.

> "The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethical committee." (#1)

> "Dicle University Hospital's Review Board in Diyarbakir, Turkey, approved the
> study. All participants gave prior written informed consent." (#2)

## 1.4 Prior work is attributed to a named author, in the sentence

The corpus almost never writes "studies show". It names the first author, then
carries the superscript reference. This is the model for fixing vague
attribution.

> "Coggon reported that subjects with a BMI>30 kg/m were 6.8 times more likely
> to develop knee OA than normal-weight controls." (#3)

> "Lohmander also found that BMI was significantly associated with the
> incidence of knee (RR 8.1) and hip (RR 2.6) arthroplasty with a continuous
> dose-response relationship between BMI and arthroplasty risk." (#3)

> "Holliday found that those who became overweight earlier in adulthood showed
> higher risks of lower limb OA." (#3)

> "Lal from Chandigarh, India, interviewed parents of 85 asthmatic children and
> found that nearly 50 per cent hesitated accepting diagnosis of asthma for
> their children." (#9)

> "Dilek showed that the thrombocytopenia and leucopenia returned to the normal
> range within one week, while anaemia took 3-4 wk." (#2)

> "Sleath reported that only 13 per cent children and 33 per cent
> parents/care-providers asked questions regarding asthma management to
> treating physician during their consultation visit." (#9)

Where a named author is genuinely unavailable, the corpus names the *place* or
the *design* instead, never a bare "studies":

> "In two studies from Kuwait and Iran, more than 80 per cent of the patients
> were found to be below the age of 40 yr." (#2)

> "A hospital-based study from Bengaluru, India, had shown steadily increase in
> asthma prevalence in children below 18 yr of age." (#9)

> "A single large population-based cohort study by Reijman investigated the
> relationship between BMI and both incidence and progression of both
> radiological knee and hip OA with a mean follow up time of 6.6 years." (#3)

## 1.5 Discussion moves study by study: prior finding, then "In our study, …"

Each Discussion paragraph pairs an external result with the present one. The
connector is literal.

> "In two studies from Kuwait and Iran, more than 80 per cent of the patients
> were found to be below the age of 40 yr. In our study, 68.8 per cent out of
> the 484 patients were below the age of 40 yr and the ratios between the
> cytopenic and non-cytopenic groups were similar." (#2)

> "In a study conducted in Turkey, 43 per cent of the patients had anaemia, 14
> per cent had thrombocytopenia, 12 per cent had leucopenia … In our study
> also, 21.5 per cent of the 484 patients had anaemia, 18.8 per cent had
> thrombocytopenia, 14.6 per cent had leucopenia…" (#2)

> "Our sample of students had higher mean depression, anxiety and stress scores
> compared with previously published data using DASS subscales." (#1)

> "Consistent with other studies female respondents had reported higher scores
> of depression, anxiety and stress compared with their male counterparts." (#1)

> "Similar findings have been reported earlier." (#1)

## 1.6 Causal explanation is offered, hedged once, and owned

The corpus does not refuse to explain. It proposes one mechanism, hedges it
once, and moves on. Contrast this with reflexive stacked hedging.

> "The reason for this could be that females are more frequently occupied with
> animal husbandry than males." (#2)

> "This may be attributed to greater fear of not attaining their goal of being
> a doctor or may be due to excessive load of both paraclinical and clinical
> subjects as compared to only clinical subjects in the latter years." (#1)

> "This may be due to the fact that women articulate depressive symptoms, even
> minor ones, more easily." (#1)

> "Contrary to earlier findings, the association of abuse of alcohol or the
> habit of smoking was not associated with any of the morbidity, which could be
> due to small number of respondents." (#1)

> "Significant additional co-morbid conditions may have contributed to longer
> LOS in this group of patients and this will also be reflected in the
> treatment costs." (#2)

> "This increase in asthma prevalence may be due to demographic changes over
> the time as suggested by increase in industries, increase in pollution and
> increased automobiles along with increased air population." (#9)

> "such ethnic variation may be attributed to the variations in study design,
> definition of hypertension, methods of blood pressure recording, age range,
> sample size, ethnicity and socio-economic class" (#12)

Pattern: `The reason for this could be that X` / `This may be attributed to X`
/ `X may have contributed to Y`. One qualifier per sentence. A named mechanism,
not "a complex interplay of factors".

## 1.7 The conclusion restates flatly, then recommends something specific

> "In conclusion, our study showed that the epidemiological, clinical and
> treatment features and relapse rates of adult brucellosis patients with
> cytopenia and those without cytopenia were similar. … It is recommended that
> patients with cytopenia should be investigated for brucellosis, especially if
> living in, or with a history of travel to, endemic areas, in view of the
> increase in world travel." (#2)

> "In conclusion, more than half of the medical undergraduate students were
> found to be affected by depression, anxiety and stress. There is a need for
> the counselling services to be made available to the students in the medical
> college to control this morbidity." (#1)

> "To conclude, asthma is common in childhood and it is underdiagnosed and
> undertreated. … At the same time, a policy should be adapted for periodical
> training of healthcare professionals dealing with asthmatic children to make
> them abreast with recent evidence-based guidelines." (#9)

Every recommendation in the corpus names an actor and an action. Counselling
services, for medical colleges. Investigation for brucellosis, by clinicians
seeing cytopenia. Periodical training, by policy. This is the working
counter-example to "further research is needed".

## 1.8 Correspondence and author replies are courteous and concrete

> "We appreciate the author for his valuable comments on our article and also
> for his concerns over some of the issues including study limitations, gender
> differences in distribution of blood pressure and prevalence of
> hypertension." (#12)

> "We acknowledge pertinent comments from Alavian on our article." (#13)

> "We agree with Alavian that HBV nucleic-acid testing (NAT) may not be
> feasible in Vietnam today." (#13)

# Part 2. Mechanical conventions

These are the checkable, near-mechanical house rules. They are what most
visibly marks a manuscript as IJMR-styled.

## 2.1 `per cent` in running prose, `%` only inside parentheses

The split is consistent across the corpus.

Narrative text spells it out:

> "In our study, 68.8 per cent out of the 484 patients were below the age of 40
> yr." (#2)

> "The variables, which were used in regression, explained only 7.7 per cent of
> depression, 7.9 per cent of anxiety and 9.1 per cent of stress." (#1)

> "Currently, nearly 10 per cent of the population is affected and the
> prevalence increases with age." (#3)

> "In 2013, of the estimated 382 million people with diabetes globally, more
> than 80 per cent lived in LMIC." (#5)

> "Nearly 80 per cent of all premature deaths due to air pollution are because
> of cardiovascular diseases." (#7)

> "Type 2 diabetes which accounts for 85-95 per cent of all diabetes has a
> latent, asymptomatic period of sub-clinical stages" (#5)

Parentheses after a count use the symbol:

> "Among these patients, 233 (48.1%) were male and 251 (51.9%) were female." (#2)

> "Of the 484 patients, 285 (60.9%) were identified as having the acute form,
> 105 (21.7%) the sub-acute form and 84 (17.4%) the chronic form of the
> disease." (#2)

> "Among the 353 respondents, 145 (41.1%) were males." (#1)

Rule: `n (xx.x%)` immediately after a count. `xx.x per cent` everywhere else.

## 2.2 Time units are abbreviated: `yr`, `wk`, `h`

> "The mean age of males was 21.38 ± 1.71 yr and that of females was 20.43 ±
> 1.37 yr." (#1)

> "brucellosis was classified as acute (less than eight weeks), sub-acute (8-52
> wk) and chronic (>52 wk)" (#2)

> "A 12 yr old boy presented…" (#10)

> "It starts with memory loss, spatial disorientation in people above the age
> of 65 yr" (#6)

> "Screening for gestational diabetes (GDM) at 24-28 wk of gestation is
> recommended" (#5)

> "Fasting and 2 h post glucose tests can identify impaired fasting glucose"
> (#5)

> "Only patients over the age of 14 yr with brucellosis were enrolled." (#2)

Note that `weeks` is spelled out when it appears in narrative prose without a
numeral pair ("less than eight weeks", "within six months"), and abbreviated in
ranges and parentheses. Follow the corpus, not a blanket rule.

## 2.3 Spelling: British `-ae-`, `-oe-`, `-our`, with Oxford `-ize`

This mix is deliberate house style. Do not "correct" it in either direction.

British forms in the corpus: `haemoglobin`, `haemophagocytosis`, `leucocyte`,
`leucopenia`, `anaemia`, `aetiology`, `paediatric`, `paediatricians`,
`dyslipidaemia`, `glycaemic`, `hyperglycaemia`, `oedema`, `programme`,
`behaviour`, `behavioural`, `tumour`, `sulphur`, `sulphonylureas`,
`haematological`, `foetal`, `centres`, `defences`, `favoured`.

Oxford `-ize` forms in the same articles: `analyzed`, `normalization`,
`randomized`, `organized`, `standardized`, `mechanization`, `computerization`,
`characterized`, `emphasizing`, `hospitalization`, `immunization`.

> "The data were analyzed with SPSS v20.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY)."
> (#1). Note `analyzed` alongside `haemoglobin` in the same paper.

## 2.4 Numbers that open a sentence are spelled out

> "One hundred and four (21.5%) had anaemia, 88 (18.8%) had thrombocytopenia,
> 71 (14.6%) had leucopenia and 28 (5.8%) had pancytopenia." (#2)

Note the rest of the series stays in numerals. Only the sentence-initial number
changes.

## 2.5 Descriptive statistics have a fixed shape

> "Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency and percentage for
> categorical variables or as mean±standard deviation (SD) and median
> [interquartile range (IQR)] for continuous variables according to the results
> of normality tests, as appropriate." (#2)

Mean and SD are set closed up in Results:

> "Their mean age±SD was 33.88±14.96 yr" (#2)
> "The mean age of cytopenic patients was 35.01±16.05 yr and 33.31±14.39 yr in
> patients without cytopenia" (#2)

Medians carry the IQR in parentheses, with the unit last:

> "the median (IQR) haemoglobin level was 11.2 (12.9-14.0) g/dl, the median
> leucocyte count was 4.4 (3.2-6.7)×10/l and the mean platelet count was
> 180±103×10/l" (#2)

> "In the cytopenic group, median restoration time of cytopenia was 14.0
> (9.0-18.5) days" (#2)

> "The median duration of medical treatment in all patients was six weeks (6-8
> wk) (maximum 24 wk), and the median LOS was nine days (7-12 days) (maximum 38
> days)." (#2)

Ranges use a plain hyphen: `8-52 wk`, `10-70 per cent`, `2-3 wk`, `85-95 per
cent`, `24-28 wk`.

## 2.6 Every instrument, reagent and package carries vendor and place

> "The data were analyzed with SPSS v20.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY)." (#1)

> "Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS for Windows v.16.5 (SPSS
> Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software package." (#2)

> "the blood samples were inoculated into BacT/ALERT FA plus aerobic bottles
> (bioMérieux, France) and were analyzed in the BacT/ALERT 3D (bioMérieux,
> France) automated blood culture system" (#2)

> "strain 99 was used for the agglutination test, which was produced in Pendik
> Animal Diseases Research Institute (Istanbul, Turkey)." (#2)

> "One such system (Medtronic's MiniMed 670G, USA) has been approved by the US
> Food and Drug Administration" (#8)

Every named statistical test appears explicitly. No "appropriate tests were
used":

> "Normality testing was done by the one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For
> group comparisons, the Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used to
> compare the categorical variables, and numerical data were analyzed using
> Student's t test for the parametric data and the Mann-Whitney U-test for the
> variables of the non-parametric data." (#2)

> "Means were compared using Student t test and ANOVA. Correlations between
> continuous variables were calculated using Pearson's correlation test.
> Multivariable step-wise linear regression analysis was carried out to find
> the role of each significant variable in determining the relevant sub-scale
> scores." (#1)

## 2.7 Headings and titles use `&`, and `Material` is singular

Structured abstract headings:

> `Background & objectives:` / `Methods:` / `Results:` /
> `Interpretation & conclusions:` (#2)

`Interpretation & conclusions` is the canonical IJMR fourth heading. Some
papers use plain `Conclusions:` (#1). Prefer the canonical form.

Body headings, in order:

`Material & Methods` (singular *Material*), `Results`, `Discussion`.

Short papers merge the last two:

> `Material & Methods` … `Results & Discussion` (#1)

Titles replace `and` with `&`:

> "Obesity & osteoarthritis" (#3)
> "Childhood diabetes mellitus: Advances & challenges" (#8)
> "Stress, anxiety & depression among medical undergraduate students & their
> socio-demographic correlates" (#1)

## 2.8 Citations are Vancouver superscript numerals

Numbered by first appearance, superscript in text, no author-date. Author
surnames appear in the sentence (see 1.4) with the numeral trailing.

# Part 3. Register

## 3.1 What the corpus does not contain

Across all 14 articles, in body prose:

- No em dashes.
- No `**bold**` or `*italic*` used for emphasis.
- No "delve", "leverage", "tapestry", "realm", "navigate" (figurative),
  "foster", "intricate", "multifaceted".
- No "It is important to note that", "Notably", "Crucially".
- "paradigm shift" appears once (#7), used literally about a change in public
  health strategy, not as decoration.

## 3.2 The evaluative vocabulary is tiny

The strongest evaluative words in the whole corpus:

> "The trend is worrying: over the past 30 years, worldwide obesity has more
> than doubled." (#3)

> "This is unfortunate because recognition of early symptoms can help to get
> the disease under control immediately" (#5)

> "Initiation of school-based heart health curriculum in Iraq is a commendable
> work" (#12)

> "Interestingly, they found the relationship for BMI and knee OA to be
> significantly stronger in women than men" (#3)

`Interestingly` is the one signposting adverb native to the register, and it is
used sparingly, attached to a specific result. It is not a licence for
"Notably" and "Importantly".

## 3.3 Sentences are long, flat and factual

The register is not punchy. Clauses accumulate. Information density is high and
the writer does not pause to tell the reader that something matters.

> "Transmission to humans is known to occur through the ingestion of
> contaminated meat and dairy products, infected animal tissue, direct contact
> of blood or bodily fluids with broken skin or conjunctiva and inhalation of
> infectious aerosols." (#2)

> "Screening programmes for CAD risk factors identify at risk people, who would
> benefit from early counselling and treatment." (#7)

> "Since the disease may involve a number of organs and systems in the body, it
> manifests with various symptoms. There are no disease-specific clinical,
> haematological or biochemical characteristics that help distinguish the
> condition from other infectious diseases. Therefore, its diagnosis may
> present a challenge." (#2)

Note the last example: three short sentences, and the connector is a plain
`Therefore`. The corpus uses `Therefore`, `However`, `Hence`, `In addition`,
`Furthermore` as ordinary logical connectors, not as paragraph-opening tics.

## 3.4 Limitations are stated in the sentence, not deferred

> "However, the proportion of obese individuals in this cohort was low,
> limiting the ability to detect increased incidence and progression
> attributable to this factor for both knee and hip OA." (#3)

> "However, weight was estimated by self-report after the surgery, possibly
> skewing the results." (#3)

> "Joint replacement data should be interpreted with care as an outcome measure
> as uptake of elective joint replacement surgery is highly influenced by
> patient and doctor preferences, socio-economic factors such as waiting lists
> and access to private healthcare, and being overweight or obese." (#3)

> "We used Centers for Disease Control (CDC) 2000 dataset for boys and girls to
> identify body mass index (BMI) percentiles instead of Indian standard due to
> non availability of such datasets from India. Hence, there may be some amount
> of deviation from the reality in terms of prevalence of hypertension,
> distribution of blood pressure and correlates of hypertension." (#12)

# Part 4. Do not copy this

The corpus is the model for *language*, not for *reporting rigour*. These
observed practices are defects. The skill's IRON RULES and Pattern 26 override
them, and the `ijmr` profile does not switch them off.

## 4.1 P values reported bare, with no effect size or confidence interval

Throughout #2, comparisons are reported with a P value alone:

> "the cytopenic patient group outnumbered the other (33 vs 28; <0.001)"

> "the LOS was significantly longer in the cytopenic group (9 vs 10 days;
> <0.001)"

> "headache was found to be more frequent in the cytopenic group (<0.01), while
> backache was more common in the non-cytopenic group (<0.001)"

> "The groups were found to differ significantly for these parameters (<0.001
> for all comparisons)."

No effect size. No 95 per cent confidence interval. The direction is given, the
magnitude is not.

**Override:** report direction, magnitude, precision (95% CI), then the exact P
as an adjunct. Write `P = 0.01`, not `P < 0.05`. Write `P < 0.001`, not
`P = 0.000`. The confidence interval is the message.

#3 shows the corpus can do this properly when the source literature supplies it,
so the good form is also attestable:

> "a 5-unit increase in body mass index was associated with a 35 per cent
> increased risk of knee OA (RR: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.21-1.51) and an 11 per cent
> increased risk of hip OA (RR: 1.11; 95% CI: 1.07-1.16)" (#3)

> "the pooled odds ratio for developing OA was 2.63 (2.28, 3.05) for obese
> subjects compared to normal-weight controls" (#3)

Use #3's form, not #2's.

## 4.2 Limitations folded into a clause instead of given a paragraph

Neither #1 nor #2 has a limitations section. #1 disposes of its sample-size
limitation inside a subordinate clause and never returns to it:

> "Contrary to earlier findings, the association of abuse of alcohol or the
> habit of smoking was not associated with any of the morbidity, which could be
> due to small number of respondents." (#1)

#2 does the same for its confounding problem:

> "Significant additional co-morbid conditions may have contributed to longer
> LOS in this group of patients and this will also be reflected in the treatment
> costs." (#2)

Both are real limitations, and in both papers the reader has to find them inside
a Discussion sentence. Contrast #3, which does give limitations their own
standing (see §3.4), and #12, which states one plainly in a reply to
correspondence.

STROBE item 19 asks for a discussion of limitations, including sources of
potential bias and imprecision, and their direction and magnitude. Keep the
paragraph. Note that §1.6 and §3.4 are still the right models for *how* to word
a limitation; the defect here is placement and completeness, not phrasing.

## 4.3 No participant flow

Neither #1 nor #2 reports screened to eligible to enrolled to analysed. #1 gives
a non-response rate for questionnaire items ("The mean non-response rate to
questions of DASS scale was 3.26 ± 2.1 per cent") but never accounts for
students who did not participate. #2 says "A total of 484 consecutive
brucellosis patients over a period of five years were enrolled" with no
denominator screened.

**Override:** the first Results paragraph is participant flow, accounting for
every participant with reasons for loss.

## 4.4 Very long unbroken paragraphs

#9 runs one paragraph from "Poor control of asthma is because of poor adherence
to therapy, parental misconceptions, variable childhood phenotypes, improper use
of drug and devices and variable asthma management practices" all the way to
"checking drug and device technique at every visit are equally crucial to keep
asthma under control in children". Between those two sentences it reports the
Lal interview study, an unnamed Delhi interview study, the same Delhi study's
findings on children, and the Sleath study, each with its own list of
percentages, without a single break. #6 has several paragraphs of comparable
length.

This is a production habit, not a virtue. Break at each change of subject: one
paragraph per study, or per claim.

## 4.5 Sentence-level infelicities present in the published text

The corpus contains typographical and grammatical slips that survived
production, for example "In this corss-sectional survey" (#1) and "increase in
pollution and increased automobiles along with increased air population" (#9,
apparently for "air pollution"). Match the register, not the errors.
